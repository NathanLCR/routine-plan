Key Concepts

  • Considerations vs issues — _considerations_ = you actively thought about ethics (mandatory for everyone); _issues_ = problems that actually surfaced (you can legitimately have none if you considered everything). Saying "no ethical considerations" = admitting you ignored ethics.
  • Research ethics (4 framing definitions) — (1) moral precepts governing research on human/animal subjects; (2) honesty, respect for participants' rights, harm avoidance; (3) guidelines and practices — a real rulebook you must find, not invent; (4) ML-specific: societal implications, fairness, accountability of deployed models. Pick the angle that fits your project.
  • Guidelines exist — you don't make them up — every field (robotics, surveys, manual handling, medical data) has published international guidelines/handbooks. Not knowing them = not knowing the rulebook. DBS has survey-ethics guidance on its site.
  • Consent ≠ availability — a dataset being downloadable does not mean you have consent. You must check the dataset description: is it open-source / licensed, or leaked / mistakenly public? "Open source" stated = consent; silence = you may not have consent.
  • Reverse-identification harm — combining quasi-identifiers (job + age + gender + region) can re-identify individuals. Anonymisation/pseudonymisation must survive a malicious reverse-engineer, and outputs must never be traceable to a person.
  • Fairness / bias — biased or imbalanced data → discriminatory models. Decide your imbalance strategy (SMOTE, over/under-sampling, augmentation for images) this semester, in the plan, not reactively next semester.
  • Accountability + explainable AI — for any wrong decision (denied loan, accident) you must be able to explain why. "The AI said no" is not accountability. Set an acceptable accuracy/error threshold _in advance_, justified by references/guidelines — never accept a random post-hoc accuracy.
  • Transparency / security — can stakeholders understand the model's judgments? Are there adversarial weaknesses that could rig results?
  • Beneficence / societal good — must beat what already exists. Copying or under-performing an existing product is unethical (no societal good; ties to IP). Novelty can be small (combine datasets, better features) but must add value.
  • Social & environmental impact (slide in red — emphasised) — simulate 1M+ users: jobs displaced, inequality, energy/compute footprint. Balance upside vs downside; if downside ≥ upside, don't do the project. Don't hide negatives in the proposal.
  • Risk assessment — table of risks × likelihood × severity, each with a mitigation / contingency. High-severity + low-likelihood is fine if justified. Exposing risks shows you thought it through; funders reject proposals whose risks they spot but you didn't.
  • Assessment cycle — ethics/risk is iterative (Define → Select/Design → Implement → Gather → Analyse → Identify gaps → Make decisions → redefine). New risks surface as you read more (papers' "future work" / failures = your risks).
  • Presentation = anti-AI verification — the viva exists so he can confirm the work is yours. Expect "what is overfitting?", "what does recall mean and why use it?" — fundamentals that expose ghostwriting/ChatGPT.

Notes (live capture)

Why ethics matters (the industry frame):

  • At company scale ethics is the first gate. His Enterprise-Ireland blood/breath project took 6+ months of ethics approval (40-page document, safety board, recruitment ages, data handling) before any data collection.
  • University ethics boards fast-track no-human-subject work but scrutinise human-subject work heavily. Failure → lawsuits, reputational collapse. "It's usually one person who sinks the company."
  • The self-checkout example: the tech existed for decades; deployment waited on the ethics/societal calculus (jobs vs efficiency), not the technology.

Data sourced from the internet (applies directly to you):

  • Data Privacy & Consent — respect privacy even for public data; confirm explicit authorisation/licence.
  • Fairness & Bias in Data — online sources are biased/inaccurate by nature (platform demographics, curation). Identify and reduce. Also check the data wasn't synthetically manipulated/poisoned by whoever uploaded it — you inherit responsibility for conclusions drawn from it.
  • Beneficence & Social Good — produce insight that helps, not a show-off reproduction.
  • Retaining Trust & Integrity — adoption of findings depends on trust built by ethical conduct.

Special ethics for ML (slide 7): Fairness & Bias; Openness & Definability (explainability to users/regulators); Security (adversarial robustness, result integrity); Impact on Social & Environmental Domains (red — environment, inequality, employment; mitigation strategies); Ongoing Assessment (continuous monitoring, retraining, feedback).

Imbalance — class question worked through live: 80/20 male/female training data → model under-learns the minority class → real-world misclassification skews toward the majority. Accuracy/confusion matrix can look fine on the small test sample but fails at 1M-user scale; even a 60/40 imbalance surfaces in deployment. Fixes: SMOTE, over/under-sampling, deliberately discarding majority samples, image augmentation (rotation, flips, crops) — all decided in the plan, with reasons.

Beneficence examples: house-price prediction where real prices are already public = no societal good; a movie recommender competing with Netflix needs a niche/differentiator. "Masters means you produce something novel others can learn from," not an undergrad reproduction.

Risk assessment mechanics (the deliverable):

  • Start as a table, then a likelihood × severity matrix (red/amber/green). Each row: risk → cause → mitigation → severity → likelihood.
  • Example row — risk: not finishing on time; severity high; likelihood low (you have the time); mitigation: follow the Gantt chart + regular supervisor meetings. (Saying likelihood "medium" implies you're planning to fail.)
  • Example row — algorithms don't work / underfit; severity high; likelihood low because chosen from literature with 10–15 supporting references (random choices = high likelihood). This is why "why these algorithms?" matters.
  • Mitigations that double as method: 3 algorithms (hedge underfitting), cross-validation + balanced data (overfitting), early start (timeline). Overfitting is a guaranteed viva question.
  • High-risk projects aren't banned — they just need more rigorous risk assessment and contingencies.

Formatting (Ch.2, echoing W5):

  • Include the equations behind every algorithm you use. Absent equations = "you don't know them" → you'll be questioned. Popular algorithms (e.g. decision trees) may omit equations in papers because they're assumed — get them from a textbook.
  • Use pseudocode for complex/novel procedures; never screenshot code in the report (undergrad habit). Code goes to an appendix / GitHub. Reason: the report must be language-agnostic (an R reader shouldn't be thrown by Python).
  • Tables for hyperparameters and per-module settings (the W5 DOE tables). EDA to justify the data.

Communication skills (slide 10): Oral (presentation skills, audience awareness, critical listening, body language), Written (academic writing, revision/editing, critical reading, data presentation), Non-verbal (audience awareness, personal presentation, body language). Non-verbal is graded in the presentation (e.g. on your phone while presenting = marked down).

Presentation skills — the script (his personal recipe, the high-value part):

  • Timing is everything. 5 minutes = hard stop at 5:00. If he stops you before results/conclusion, those sections score zero. Record and rehearse; first attempt always overruns (~2×).
  • 1-minute-per-slide rule → a 5-min talk = 5–7 slides. More slides = useless/confusing.
  • Opening statement (first ~30–60s): state problem → motivation → solution → conclusion, high-level, no jargon/acronyms. Like a trailer / court opening statement — someone who steps out and returns still knows what it's about. Don't hide the conclusion for suspense.
  • Hook — explain the problem well and why it must be solved; remind throughout. 80% of failures come from skipping this and rushing to the solution.
  • Literature backing (the most-violated item — likely going red next year): even 5–10 seconds. Name a lead author + where they're from ("Frank and colleagues, 2025"); a table of "what the literature said → gap → my RQs" works. ~30 seconds, but it must be there.
  • Methods shorter than results+discussion. Use tables for methods/hyperparameters. Spend most time on results & discussion together (you have no results yet → talk about the proposed solution, evaluation metrics, ethics, what a good result looks like).
  • Slides = mostly figures/tables, not text. Unlike a lecture deck, the audience should need you to interpret them.
  • Conclusion (graded column) — reinforce the opening: "the problem I said mattered, I solved." Not bullet points read aloud. Psychologically ties the marker to a high score.
  • Academic grading = rubric columns (problem, literature, methods, results, conclusion, delivery), not "good vibes" — introverts can score 10/10 by ticking boxes. (5 Ps from the slide: Project voice, Pronounce, Pace slightly faster, Pitch varied, Pause for emphasis.)

Flashcards (SR)

  • Q: Difference between ethical _considerations_ and ethical _issues_?

    • A: Considerations = you actively thought about ethics (mandatory for everyone). Issues = problems that actually arose (you can have none if you considered everything). "No considerations" = you ignored ethics.

  • Q: Does a downloadable dataset imply consent to use it?

    • A: No. Check the description/licence — "open source/licensed" = consent; silence could mean leaked or mistakenly public. Provenance must be traced.

  • Q: What's wrong with accepting whatever accuracy your model gives?

    • A: No accountability. Set an acceptable accuracy/error threshold in advance, justified by references/guidelines.

  • Q: Why is an 80/20 class imbalance an ethics problem, not just a metrics problem?

    • A: The model under-learns the minority class → discriminatory misclassification at scale, even when test-set accuracy looks fine. Decide a balancing strategy in the plan.

  • Q: What three things does each risk-matrix row need?

    • A: Likelihood, severity, and a mitigation/contingency (plus cause). High-severity + low-likelihood is acceptable if justified.

  • Q: 1-minute-per-slide rule → how many slides for a 5-minute talk?

    • A: 5–7 slides. More = useless/confusing.

  • Q: What four things go in the opening statement, and in how long?

    • A: Problem, motivation, solution, conclusion — ~30–60 seconds, high-level, no jargon. Like a trailer / court opening.

  • Q: Which presentation section do students most often skip, costing the most marks?

    • A: Literature backing — name one lead author + the gap, ~30 seconds; never present as if you're the first to study it.

  • Q: Where should most of the 5 minutes go?

    • A: Results & discussion together (or, pre-results, the proposed solution + evaluation metrics + ethics). Methods should be shorter.

  • Q: Why does the viva/presentation exist at all "these days"?

    • A: To verify the work is yours (anti-ghostwriting/anti-AI). Expect fundamentals: "what is overfitting?", "what does recall mean and why use it?"
