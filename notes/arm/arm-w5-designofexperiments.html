ARM — Week 5: Design of Experiments (Chapter 2)

Focus

Chapter 2 (methodology) is a recipe book: someone who has never seen your project must be able to reproduce it exactly — every method, every setting, every measurement. No theorizing (that was Chapter 1), no results (that's Chapter 3+). The core of the week is Design of Experiments (DOE): experiments are designed, never random. You anchor every hyperparameter range in literature, name your design type (the lecturer recommends full factorial, the formal equivalent of grid search), and reduce bias through randomization, replication, and blocking. Deliverable: a DOE table per algorithm (hyperparameters × levels, ranges justified with ≥3 paper references) — due next week, fully done, for all algorithms, pasted in the Google Doc. Key Concepts

  • Chapter 2 = recipe — exact methods, exact settings, exact measurements; reproducible by a stranger; no theory, no results, no Python graphs
  • Experiment — a controlled, named technique testing a hypothesis; if you can't name the design type, it's not controlled
  • Four definitions (pick the one that speaks to you): controlled technique / manipulate independent → observe dependent variables / cause-and-effect with other variables held constant (→ explainable AI) / structured strategy to advance understanding + find causal linkages
  • Five reasons to experiment: find causal linkages (EDA), evaluate/disprove theories (fair algorithm comparison), empirical evidence for decisions, uncover factors for optimization, refine hypotheses (cyclic — go back and redesign)
  • Bias reduction triad — randomization (run order not hand-picked), replication (≥3 runs, report mean ± std; cross-validation is replication), blocking (same machine, same conditions). All three must appear in the proposal
  • Degrees of freedom — number of independent things you can change; know yours
  • Full factorial design — all combinations; 3 factors × 3 levels = 3³ = 27 experiments; grid search is full factorial — but report all 27 results and effects, not just the winner
  • Fractional factorial ≈ random search — faster, and by not chasing the absolute optimum it can avoid overfitting
  • Other named designs: response surface, Plackett–Burman, central composite, Taguchi, Latin square, randomized complete block (each has rules — pick one, follow it, name it)
  • Grid-search trap — optimizing accuracy alone hides overfitting; plotting effects (epochs vs accuracy, trees vs accuracy) lets you trade a little training accuracy for generalization
  • Defaults are not an excuse — a student failed for not knowing sklearn's default tree count/depth; even unchanged settings must be known, reported, and justified
  • Three experiment families for the thesis: hyperparameter tuning (everyone), feature engineering (some), algorithm comparison (everyone — fairness must be designed)
  • 3 algorithms = sweet spot (worst / middle / best); fewer needs heavy justification, more = unmastered work
  • Feature reduction reasons: model efficiency (noise/correlated features), memory/training time, deployment cost (MRI vs age+gender), practicality (long surveys get abandoned)
  • Novelty via DOE — extend the literature's design space (they stopped at 500 trees, you test up to 1,000) and report the effect, even a null one

Notes (live capture)

Chapter 2 tone and content:

  • You are no longer theorizing or introducing. You state exactly what you'll do, how, and how it's measured.
  • Problem statement, motivation, literature = Chapter 1. Results, graphs = later chapters. Neither belongs here.
  • Methods: which algorithms, why selected, how they work, how they'll be tuned. Selection rationale was seeded in Ch.1 (what the literature uses); here it's operational.
  • If a reader can't reproduce your work from Chapter 2 alone, the chapter is badly written — that's on you, not the reader.

The experiment cycle:

  • Hypothesis → theory around it → phenomenon (run it) → results → back to refine hypothesis/design. Most students treat it as one-shot; submitting a 50% accuracy with no iteration loop visible = likely fail. Low accuracy is acceptable only after demonstrably exhausting the design space.

Engineering view:

  • Process with inputs → outputs. Inputs split into controllable factors (you manipulate) and uncontrollable factors (you must at least know them). Anything you don't control needs a stated reason.

Bias reduction (must be in next week's proposal):

  • Randomization — don't pick experiment order; matters most in physical experiments (don't sample all men first), still state it.
  • Replication — run everything ≥3 times. K-fold CV is replication. The meticulous version: report 60% ± 2%. A single Python number is not a result.
  • Blocking — remove batch/day/shift effects; for us: same computer, same processing conditions for every run, so nothing gets an advantage.

Full factorial worked example (random forest):

  • From 4–6 papers, harvest the tuned hyperparameters: n_trees, max_depth, min_samples_leaf.
  • Find the literature range (e.g. trees 1–100, best ≈50, below 20 useless) → design your own informed range (20–200), pick 3–5 equally spaced levels.
  • 3 factors × 3 levels = 27 experiments — the minimum start. Aim for 5×5 if you can explain it. Don't invent 20×20 — if no paper does it, there's a reason.
  • Grid search hands you 1 result; you actually generated 27. Analyzing the other 26 (effect plots, interactions, R²) is where explainability and novelty live.
  • Possible finding: "trees don't affect accuracy here" → use the fewest trees → smaller, cheaper, faster model at equal accuracy. You only learn this by looking at all combinations.
  • Worst case: nothing is significant → back to the drawing board, choose factors that matter.

Full factorial — pros/cons:

  • Pros: comprehensive; identifies significant factors without oversight; robust general conclusions; quantifies interactions (R²); flexible.
  • Cons: large sample needs (cheap for simulations, expensive for gold nanoparticles); demands time; requires careful planning (decide what stays constant, maybe via preliminary runs); higher-order nonlinear interactions may need SHAP/LIME-style explanation.

Math / Code

Full factorial size:  n_experiments = levels ^ factors
  3 factors × 3 levels  → 3³  = 27   ("3×3 full factorial DOE" — minimum)
  5 factors × 5 levels  → 5⁵  = 3,125 (fine for simulation; must be analyzable)

Replication: every cell run k≥3 times → report mean ± std (CV counts).

DOE table template (one per algorithm):
  | Hyperparameter      | Levels (equally spaced)   | Literature range | Refs    |
  |---------------------|---------------------------|------------------|---------|
  | n_estimators        | 20, 65, 110, 155, 200     | 1–100, best ≈50  | [1][2][3]|
  | max_depth           | 2, 4, 6, 8, 10            | 2–10             | [1][4]  |
  | min_samples_leaf    | 2, 5, 9, 12, 15           | 2–15             | [2][3]  |

Design name to state in Ch.2: "k×n full factorial design of experiments,
3 replicates per cell, randomized run order, fixed hardware (blocking)."

  • Q: Grid search is formally which DOE type? Random search?

    • A: Grid search = full factorial (all combinations). Random search ≈ fractional factorial (a sampled fraction — faster, can avoid overfitting).

  • Q: How many experiments in a 3×3 full factorial? Formula?

    • A: levels^factors = 3³ = 27.

  • Q: Why is taking only the grid-search winner a mistake?

    • A: You ran 27 experiments and ignored 26 — the effects, interactions, and overfitting signals. The winner on training accuracy may generalize worse than a slightly weaker setting.
