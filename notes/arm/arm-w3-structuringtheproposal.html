ARM — Week 3: Structuring Your Research Proposal

Focus

The proposal has a defined structure and each section has a real job. This week maps that structure — title → abstract → background → methodology → timescale — and introduces the two criteria that cut across everything: coherence (does it hang together?) and appropriateness (does it follow the rules?). The abstract is the last thing you write and the first thing that gets judged; the title directly affects your grade and your citation count. Data set submission is Week 7 — that is four weeks away, not "later".

Key Concepts

  • Proposal structure — title → abstract → background → methodology → timescale
  • Title criteria — clarity/precision, relevance/significance, keywords/searchability
  • Title trap — if a fancy word is in the title, the entire report must deliver on it
  • Abstract — 200 words max; written last; covers everything; motivates the reader
  • How to read a paper — abstract → conclusion → results → methodology (not in order)
  • Background — three functions: introduce the problem, justify the RQ/aim, ground in literature
  • Methodology — the largest section; philosophy → design → participants → techniques → ethics
  • Timescale — Gantt chart; include holidays, dependencies, all requirements (reflective diary)
  • Research Proposal Checklist (Box 2.11) — the literal marking grid; use it before submitting
  • Reflective diary — 100–200 words per entry; written every week
  • Reflective vs Reflexive — distinct concepts; both matter for the diary
  • Self-plagiarism — can't reuse submitted work, even your own; reference yourself differently
  • AI detection — tools exist beyond Turnitin; 60% similarity + submitting = fail
  • Fail ARM = no early repeat, no progression, visa risk

Where you should be at Week 3:

  • Numbers: 1–2 RQs (2 is ideal); 3 is too many and costs marks; aims ~3 is the sweet spot, 2–5 is acceptable range

The Title:

  • Job: tell the reader exactly what the study is, no tricks, no confusion
  • Three criteria from the slides:
  • Clarity and precision — succinct, captures main focus, avoids vagueness and complexity
  • Relevance and significance — signals the problem being addressed, interests the right reader
  • Keywords and searchability — how you're paired with the right supervisor; how you get cited
  • Title trap (lecture examples, real grade consequences):
  • "big data" in the title → entire report must deliver big data (five Vs, all of them)
  • "explainable AI" → entire report must justify why explainability is needed (not just SHAP + feature importance), cover all four explainability categories, measure and compare explainability across models
  • The lecturer's best paper isn't getting citations because he tried a fancy title — searchability killed it
  • Process: generate 3–5 candidate titles, test each against clarity + searchability, then trim
  • Format: one sentence ideally; subtitle allowed when length vs clarity tradeoff demands it
  • Avoid acronyms unless extremely standard

The Abstract (200 words max):

  • Written last, appears first — strange but real
  • Covers: motivation, lit overview, methods, results — everything
  • Not a dry summary: it must also motivate reading ("facts + why + how")
  • How to evaluate a paper you're considering reading: abstract → conclusion (they must match) → skip to results → methodology. Not front-to-back
  • Example abstract (housing price prediction, C4.5/RIPPER/AdaBoost) — scored 6/10:
  • Negative: first sentence is too long; the opening line should state the problem (or problem + solution) in one clear sentence
  • Negative: acronyms (OFHEO, MRIS) dropped without definition early on
  • (transcript cut off before full critique — notes are partial)
  • Why it matters: at publication scale, the abstract is the filter that decides whether your paper gets read or ignored
  • Supervisor editing insight: 30 minutes on 200 words is normal — every line is load-bearing

The Background section: Three functions (from slides):

  1. Introduce the research issue — answer "what am I going to do?" Engage the reader's interest; justify the choice (personal interest + intellectual curiosity + career direction + perceived value)
  2. Rationale for the research — answer "why am I going to do this?" Justify worthiness via one of: applying a theory to a specific context / developing a theory within a research setting / testing a theory within a given context
  3. Grounding in academic literature — show knowledge of relevant prior work; NOT a full lit review, just an overview of key sources and theories that will inform the research

The Methodology section (largest section):

  • Subsections: research philosophy, design, participants, techniques, procedures, ethical considerations
  • Research philosophy → directly determines design choice (quantitative / qualitative / mixed methods)
  • Justify and describe each element so they cohere — philosophy → design → methods must align
  • For primary data: specify participants, organisation, sector, location
  • Describe sampling technique, sample size, collection/analysis methods at overview level (not full detail)
  • Ethics — crucial for human participants AND secondary data. Show forethought. Even anonymised macro-level data warrants consideration
  • Ethical considerations may need their own dedicated section depending on the project

Timescale (Gantt chart):

  • Divide the project into distinct stages/tasks; estimate time for each
  • Gantt format: tasks in rows, weeks in columns, bar length = estimated time
  • Must include: holidays (Christmas, Easter), sequential dependencies, ALL project requirements including the reflective diary
  • Buffer rule: research takes longer than expected — update the chart regularly
  • Dataset submission = Week 7. That is ~4 weeks from now. If you don't have it by then, drop the project

Dataset vetting (from the live discussion):

  • Timer rule: sit for 1–2 hours and actually look. If you've never done that, you haven't tried
  • Kaggle is still banned; but if you can reverse-engineer the original source and access it from there, that's fine
  • What "approved dataset" means: you have read every column, know what each feature means, know which column you're predicting, checked for missing values, and can answer live questions about it
  • Image/audio datasets count. RL projects: discuss environment design with the lecturer
  • Surveys and human data: ethics approval required (4–5 week lead time) — do not collect anything before it

Reflective Diary:

  • Required weekly entry: 100–200 words
  • It's assessed and demonstrates ownership/responsibility of the research process
  • Reflective vs Reflexive (distinct — the slides define both):
  • Reflective: contemplation; looking back on experiences, actions, or knowledge; introspection; "what happened and what does it mean?"
  • Reflexive: awareness of your own position and its influence on the process; "how does my presence/bias affect the research itself?" — critical in qualitative/social science research
  • Box 1.4 checklist for diary entries:
  • In relation to each experience: what went well (why? what does it mean for the research?)? what didn't go well (why? what adjustments will you make?)?
  • Looking back: how could you have improved? what themes emerge across entries? how will you apply the learning?

Research Proposal Checklist (Box 2.11) — the actual marking grid:

  • Have I explained what I'm going to do?
  • Have I explained why I'm doing this?
  • Have I said why it's worth doing?
  • Have I explained how it relates to prior research in the area?
  • Have I stated which theory/theories inform the work and how I'll use them?
  • Have I stated my RQ(s), aim, and objectives/investigative questions?
  • Have I outlined how I'll conduct the research?
  • Have I outlined my research design?
  • Have I outlined what data I need?
  • Have I stated who my participants/respondents are and where?
  • Have I explained how I'll select them?
  • Have I explained how I'll gain access?
  • Have I outlined how I'll collect data?
  • Have I outlined how I'll analyse data and develop theoretical explanations?
  • Have I outlined potential data quality issues?
  • Have I outlined how I'll overcome those issues?
  • Have I considered ethical issues at each stage?
  • Have I outlined how I'll address those ethical issues?

Integrity warnings (non-negotiable):

  • AI writing tools are detectable — AI detection goes beyond Turnitin; tools you don't know about; a student was failed for an AI-written style section
  • Turnitin 60% similarity is already a red flag; submitting it anyway is worse — it's on record
  • Self-plagiarism: if you reuse a previous submission, Turnitin flags it at 100% match — you must reference yourself and reword
  • Fail ARM = no early repeat (unlike other modules), dissertation stops, visa implications if international

Flashcards (SR)

  • Q: In what order should you write the proposal sections?

    • A: Write the abstract last, even though it appears first. Everything else feeds into it.

  • Q: What are the three criteria for a good research title?

    • A: Clarity and precision (tells you exactly what it is), relevance and significance (signals the problem), keywords and searchability (findability and supervisor matching).

  • Q: What is the title trap and why does it cost marks?

    • A: If a technical term (e.g., "explainable AI", "big data") is in the title, the entire report must fully deliver on it. Using a term you don't fully understand or implement is exposed immediately.

  • Q: How long is an abstract and what must it contain?

    • A: 200 words maximum. It covers motivation, related literature, methods, and results — plus the "why" and "how", not just facts. It must motivate reading.

  • Q: What is the efficient order for reading a research paper?

    • A: Abstract → conclusion (they must match) → results → methodology. Do not read front-to-back.

  • Q: What are the three functions of the Background section?

    • A: (1) Introduce the research issue ("what am I doing?"), (2) provide rationale for the RQ/aim ("why am I doing this?"), (3) ground in the academic literature ("where does this fit?").

  • Q: Distinguish reflective from reflexive.

    • A: Reflective = contemplating your own experiences and actions; looking back. Reflexive = awareness of your own position/bias and how it influences the research process itself.

  • Q: What is the dataset submission deadline, and what triggers a project drop?

    • A: Week 7. If you don't have an approvable dataset by then, drop the project.

  • Q: What does dataset approval actually require?

    • A: You know every column, know what you're predicting, checked for missing values, can answer live questions. The tutor doesn't vet it — you do first.

  • Q: Why can't you reuse a section from a previous submission?

    • A: Self-plagiarism — it was submitted to Turnitin already. It will come up as 100% match against your own prior work. You must reword it and cite yourself.
