ARM — Week 1: Introduction to Applied Research

Focus

Applied research solves a specific practical problem for identifiable stakeholders — it is not about inventing algorithms or expanding theory. The module IS the dissertation preparation. The biggest risk is delaying topic choice, picking methods you don't understand, or treating the proposal as something to solve later.

Key Concepts

  • Applied research — solves a practical problem, tied to stakeholders, time-bound, immediately useful
  • Basic research — explores fundamental concepts, builds theory, academic-led, speculative timeline
  • Relevance gap — the separation between knowledge producers and the people who can use the knowledge
  • Reflexivity — researcher's awareness of their own influence on the research process
  • Reflective journal — weekly research diary tracking decisions, blockers, and lessons
  • Tsui's seven responsible research principles
  • Google Doc research file — the live dissertation/proposal draft for this module
  • Iterative research process — topics, RQs, and methods change; catching problems early is the skill

Notes (live capture)

What this module is really for:

  • It is preparation for the dissertation — leave with a proposal and a working research file
  • Students who fail or struggle: delayed topic choice, picked techniques they don't understand,

    • missed meetings, treated proposal as something to solve later

  • The dissertation does not need to invent a new algorithm — it needs to apply known methods

    • rigorously to a specific real-world problem where stakeholder value can be explained

Basic vs applied research:

  • Basic — explores fundamental concepts, discovers phenomena, builds/extends theory, creates

    • general knowledge; academic-led, longer timeline, impact starts in academia

  • Applied — solves a specific practical problem; industry/org-facing, time-bound, stakeholder-tied;

    • must be immediately useful to practitioners, managers, organisations, or a defined community

Research is iterative (not linear):

  • Topic idea may change after literature review
  • RQs may change after feasibility check
  • Methodology may change after discovering data or annotation constraints
  • Good student: catches problems early; bad pattern: delays commitment until it's too late

Bad pattern (lecturer warning):

  • Spend weeks thinking without committing
  • Pick a topic because it sounds impressive
  • Choose algorithms not understood
  • Discover too late that method or dataset is not viable

The relevance gap:

  • Separation between knowledge producers and the people who can use the knowledge
  • Research must answer: "who would use this and why?" — if this can't be answered, the work is weakly framed
  • Reduce the gap by identifying users, communicating clearly, connecting findings to practice

Reflective journal:

  • Strongly recommended by lecturer — weekly entry, five minutes is enough
  • Purpose: track decisions, blockers, lessons; prepare for viva questions; prevent self-deception about skipped weeks
  • Format: what I worked on → what went well → why → what went wrong → why → implications for RQ/method → decision → next action

Google Doc research file:

  • Create a Google Doc — this is the central file for the module
  • Add the link to the Moodle-linked Google Sheet — target: before Week 3 (~2026-05-27)
  • Share with lecturer only (plagiarism tracking)
  • Will become: Chapter 1 (intro + lit review) + Chapter 2 (methodology); also generates the

    • 2,000-word proposal, PowerPoint, and poster

Topic selection rule:

  • Choose early — ideally in the first 2–3 weeks
  • Must be: specific problem, identifiable stakeholders, existing literature, learnable methods,

    • narrow enough for 6 months, no "impressive-sounding" technique chosen without understanding it

  • Lecturer analogy: a consultant knows tools, timeline, risks, and expected outputs before starting

Tsui's seven responsible research principles:

  1. Stakeholder involvement
  2. Valuing plurality and collaboration
  3. Valuing basic and applied contributions
  4. Improving rigor and relevance
  5. Impact on stakeholders
  6. Broad dissemination
  7. Sound methodology

Flashcards (SR)

  • Q: What is the primary purpose of basic research?

    • A: Explore fundamental concepts, discover new phenomena, build or extend theory, and create general knowledge — not tied to immediate practical use.

  • Q: What makes applied research different from basic research?

    • A: Applied research solves a specific practical problem with identifiable stakeholders; it must be immediately useful and is typically time-bound and practitioner-facing.

  • Q: Why is stakeholder usefulness essential for the dissertation?

    • A: Applied research must answer "who would use this and why?" — without that, the work is weakly framed and hard to defend at viva.

  • Q: What is the relevance gap?

    • A: The separation between knowledge producers (researchers) and the people who can use the knowledge (practitioners, organisations, communities).

  • Q: Why should the research process be treated as iterative?

    • A: Topics, RQs, and methods change after literature review and feasibility checks — catching problems early is far better than discovering them at submission.

  • Q: What should go into the weekly reflective journal entry?

    • A: What I worked on, what went well and why, what went wrong and why, implications for RQ/method, any decision or adjustment made, and the next action.

  • Q: Why is it risky to choose algorithms you do not understand?

    • A: You cannot defend them at viva; failures or changes cannot be diagnosed; the proposal will not be credible because you cannot describe the method.

  • Q: What should the Google Doc become by the end of the ARM module?

    • A: A live dissertation/proposal draft with Chapter 1 (intro + lit review) and Chapter 2 (methodology), from which the 2,000-word proposal, PowerPoint, and poster are derived.

  • Q: Name Tsui's seven responsible research principles.

    • A: Stakeholder involvement, valuing plurality and collaboration, valuing basic and applied contributions, improving rigor and relevance, impact on stakeholders, broad dissemination, sound methodology.

Lecture 1 Reflection (homework — 100–200 words)

Lecture 1 clarified that applied research must solve a specific practical problem for identifiable stakeholders, not simply expand academic theory. The critical distinction is immediacy: applied research must be useful now, to a defined group, within a realistic timescale — not speculatively useful at some future point.

For my dissertation, this framing is directly relevant. I am comparing three automatic grounding evaluators — ALCE-style NLI, RAGAS faithfulness, and LLM-as-judge — against human sentence-level labels on Portuguese academic RAG outputs. The work is not inventing a new RAG architecture; it is evaluating whether existing tools can be trusted for a specific, underexplored language and domain. The stakeholders are concrete: Portuguese NLP researchers, MSc students working in Portuguese, RAG system developers, and anyone evaluating the reliability of automatically generated academic answers in Portuguese who needs to know whether these tools agree with human judgment.

The lecture also reinforced that research is iterative — committing to a defensible topic early and revising with evidence is the skill itself. This week my priority is creating the ARM Google Doc, making the first reflective journal entry, and converting my locked research questions into proposal-ready language.
