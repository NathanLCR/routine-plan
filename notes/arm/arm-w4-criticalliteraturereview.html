ARM — Week 4: The Critical Literature Review

Focus

The lit review is the anchor chapter — it sets context, justifies the work, gives you a baseline to compare results against, and comes back in methodology, results, and discussion. It is critical, not a summary pile: develop an argument, expose conflicts, find the gap. The deliverable this week is a relevance tree (the diagram at the top of the chapter that breaks the review into ~4 themed branches, each ≈10–15 papers). Plus a review paper and a citation manager (Mendeley). By next week the lecturer expects Chapter 1 half-done with real references. The whole module is now moving faster — we are a third of the way through with four weeks gone.

Key Concepts

  • Critical Literature Review — an argument, not a summary; the anchor chapter everything else revolves around
  • It returns later — baseline for the results section, justification in methodology, reference point in discussion
  • Relevance tree — diagram at the top of the lit review; topic → ~4 themed branches → ~10–15 papers each; forces strategic, targeted reading
  • Review paper — one paper covering hundreds of others in a field; read one and you map the landscape; check its future-work section
  • Anchor paper — your chosen core paper; should be highlighted, vetted, re-read; its reference list seeds your own
  • Finding a gap — sometimes the branch you can't find papers for is the novelty; let it reshape your research question
  • Six types of review: integrative, theoretical, historical, methodological, argumentative, systematic
  • Yours = systematic (planning) → integrative (bulk) + light hints of the others; no full history, no full theory, no argument
  • White vs grey sources — white = peer-reviewed journals (use these); grey = conference papers, websites, blogs, news (1–2 max, never blogs)
  • Impact factor & CiteScore — quality signals; >3 good, 5–10 great, >10 elite in AI
  • Mendeley — mandatory citation manager (Word add-in + web importer); referencing style IEEE (numeric) for this lecturer
  • 8 forms of plagiarism — incl. paraphrasing, collusion, inaccurate referencing, self-plagiarism, ghost-writing, generative AI
  • Plagiarism committee catches ~30 students/month; second offence = course suspension
  • Relevance / credibility / sufficiency — the three axes for vetting every source
  • Student workload rule: 2 hours independent study per 1 hour of class → ~4 hours owed before next week

Notes (live capture)

Where the lit review sits in Chapter 1:

  • Chapter 1 = background (top) → research questions, aims, objectives (end). The lit review is the bulk in the middle.
  • It is not a one-and-done chapter — you keep adding to it, and it reappears in results (compare your accuracy to theirs), methodology (these methods are used in the literature, so trust them), and discussion.

Why "critical" sits in front of "literature review":

  • Develop a clear argument about what is known and not known — there are sides.
  • Avoid simply summarising articles (the #1 student failure). Question conventional wisdom — which requires actually knowing the material deeply.
  • Challenge domain views, acknowledge gaps, identify unclear/biased/inconsistent concepts.
  • "Finalise" appears before everything because you decide what you're looking for before you go in — it's planned, not random.

The Relevance Tree (Task 1 — due next week, top of the lit review):

  • Worked example: topic = stock prediction, four branches:
  1. ML algorithms — top algorithms (SVM, Random Forest, ensembles), why used, which wins when (RF on noisy data? why?), challenges, limitations
  2. Datasets — size, features, feature engineering (PCA / dimensionality reduction), how they split/manage data
  3. Ethics — everyone must have this branch; general AI ethics + domain-specific (money, user profiles, losses). Big grade weight, dedicated lecture coming
  4. Single vs multiple stocks — different approaches, accuracy/performance differences and why
  • Each box ≈ 10–15 papers → ~40 papers total. Don't go crazy on branch count (3–6 is fine).
  • Focus one theme per week: "this week I read the algorithms branch, summarise + evaluate + critique, find the main insight."
  • The novelty trick: if a branch is hard to find papers for (e.g. single vs multiple stock prediction), that obvious-but-unstudied gap can become your research question. Fresh insight on an existing field = first-class territory. (Lecturer's example: a student comparing combined vs single predictions for wind/solar energy — novel framing, getting published.)
  • Build it in PowerPoint / Canva — but it's not for display; it drives which papers you actually download and read.
  • Place it right after the background: "My literature review breaks into four themes — algorithms, datasets, ethics, single-vs-multiple — as shown in Figure 1." Then Figure 1. Relevance tree. Use the branches as subheadings, or flow in prose.

Finding a review paper (Task — may already be assigned):

  • Google Scholar (biggest), also ResearchGate / normal Google. Search: a review of [your title] → e.g. "a review of stock prediction using machine learning".
  • Must have the word review in the title (systematic / comprehensive / etc.). If it doesn't, skip it.
  • You only need to read one — they cover the same references and say the same things, just worded differently. Pick a recent one (e.g. 2025) for the latest summary.
  • Gold-standard review ≈ 154 references shown in lecture; lecturer considers 200+ truly strong. Don't expect to hit that in 3 months.
  • It takes long to read but afterward you understand the literature landscape — then go deeper into original research.

The six review types (know yours):

  • Integrative — most undergrad/master's reviews; critically analyses ideas and relationships, exposes what's lacking → leads to your RQs. Most of yours.
  • Theoretical — heavy equations and proofs; mathematicians/physicists bringing a new theory. Hint only, not the bulk.
  • Historical — evolution over time with accurate dates/context (1997 LSTM → CNN → big data → 2020 ChatGPT → agents). Use techniques for emphasis (opening stat lines) but don't make it historical.
  • Methodological — focuses on research approaches, procedures, data-collection techniques; for people proposing a new method (e.g. cybersecurity). Only if you're changing a method.
  • Argumentative — you argue a clear position (current approach is wrong). Requires real expertise. Avoid at this stage — don't use words like "superior" or "the best" (ChatGPT loves them).
  • Systematic — comprehensive pre-planned strategy; you plan exactly what to look for. This is your starting/planning phase.
  • Verdict for Nathan's cohort: systematic (planning) + integrative (bulk), light theory hint (a few equations), no real history/methodology/argument.

Six purposes of a critical lit review (checklist — if yours misses one, it's incomplete):

  1. Put research in context — everything in the review must relate to what you're doing; cut anything that's just filler (don't write two paragraphs on LSTM if you're not using it)
  2. Resolve conflict — paint the conflict, then show how you resolve it (proves you read enough to find it)
  3. Discuss relevant theories — equations, diagrams, graphs, tables (the background diagram was due W3)
  4. Definitions and clarification — you're allowed teaching moments (in the background)
  5. Insights into related research your work extends or challenges — state the state of the art, then a one-paragraph, obvious novelty statement near the end
  6. Supporting evidence that your RQs/aims are worth researching — if you can't find papers about your topic, drop it (you can't convince an examiner as a student; experts' work is your evidence)

Sources — white vs grey:

  • White sources = peer-reviewed journal articles. Vetted by 2–3 anonymous expert reviewers + an editor; never accepted first time. ~90% of your thesis should be these.
  • Grey sources = conference papers, websites, news, books — useful but unvetted. 1–2 website refs max, never blogs/podcasts. Conference papers are great for learning but lightly cited.
  • Vetting quality: journal name visible on Scholar → click journal → check Impact Factor and CiteScore.
  • IF <3 = okay/low barrier; 3–5 = good; 5–10 = great (mostly Q1, hard to enter); >10 = elite in AI.
  • Example: a journal at IF 19.3, ~21% acceptance, CiteScore ~35 — extremely hard to get into.
  • Don't over-optimise on this unless you're the detail-oriented type; "it's not that deep."
  • Access: log in with DBS account (institutional subscriptions). If still paywalled, email the lecturer the DOI — he can usually retrieve it.

Plagiarism (non-negotiable):

  • DBS AI/plagiarism committee catches ~30 students/month; two offences = course suspension. They are paid to find it; they catch it in reports and exams.
  • 8 forms: (1) word-for-word quoting without acknowledgement — incl. generative AI output (LLMs reproduce experts' phrasing verbatim); (2) copy-pasting text/diagrams from the internet — for diagrams, either redraw them or copy-paste and reference the source; (3) paraphrasing (changing words, same structure/thinking — still plagiarism, detectable); (4) colluding (sharing your report with a friend — both parties penalised; protect your work); (5) inaccurate referencing (a wrong citation counts as plagiarism — hence Mendeley); (6) using ghost-writing/essay services; (7) self-plagiarism (you can't reuse your own prior submission verbatim — Turnitin flags 100% against yourself; reword + cite yourself); (8) generative AI — allowed only when explicitly permitted (planning); if AI isn't mentioned, assume you may not use it. Use it like a hammer (a tool), never to do the thinking.

Vetting axes (relevance tree quality):

  • Relevance — how recent? In AI, 2006/2010/2015 is old; restrict to last 5 years, extend another 5 only if you must. References will be checked for recency %.
  • Credibility — white vs grey, impact factor, guidance value for future research.
  • Sufficiency — "how many references?" is a bad question. You decide when a point is sufficiently supported. Two reputable sources confirming a fact may be enough; contested claims need more. No magic number.

Workload rule: full-time = 2 hours independent study per 1 hour of class. This 2-hour session → ~4 hours of your own work (relevance tree, Gantt, reading) before next week.

Chapter connections: every chapter ends with a summary that bridges to the next. Lit review → leads into Chapter 2 (methodology: parameters, design of experiments) → Chapter 3 (doing it). Towards the end of the lit review, briefly outline subsequent sections.

Flashcards (SR)

  • Q: What is the deliverable due at the top of the lit review next week?

    • A: A relevance tree — a diagram breaking the topic into ~4 themed branches, each ≈10–15 papers, that drives targeted reading.

  • Q: Why is the lit review called the "anchor" chapter?

    • A: It sets context and justifies the work, and reappears later — as the baseline in results, the justification in methodology, and the reference point in discussion.

  • Q: What single mistake do most students make in the lit review?

    • A: Summarising articles instead of building a critical argument — no analysis, no conflict, no gap.

  • Q: How many review papers do you need to read, and why?

    • A: One. Reviews in the same field cover the same references and reach the same conclusions; one maps the landscape (then read original research for depth).

  • Q: Which two review types should yours be, and which to avoid?

    • A: Systematic (planning) + integrative (bulk). Avoid full historical, theoretical, methodological, and argumentative — use only light hints.

  • Q: White vs grey sources?

    • A: White = peer-reviewed journal articles (≈90% of the thesis). Grey = conference papers, websites, news (1–2 max), never blogs/podcasts.

  • Q: What impact factor counts as good vs elite in AI?

    • A: >3 good, 5–10 great (mostly Q1), >10 elite. Use CiteScore too. Don't over-optimise.

  • Q: Why is an inaccurate citation a serious problem?

    • A: Wrong references count as plagiarism. Use Mendeley (IEEE numeric style) and double-check output.

  • Q: What is self-plagiarism and how do you avoid it?

    • A: Reusing your own prior submission verbatim — Turnitin flags 100% against yourself. Reword and cite yourself.

  • Q: When deciding "how many references is enough"?

    • A: There's no magic number. Sufficiency depends on the claim — reputable agreement on a fact needs few; contested claims need more.

  • Q: What does the "hard-to-find branch" often signal?

    • A: A real gap — the obvious-but-unstudied question that can become your novel research question.
