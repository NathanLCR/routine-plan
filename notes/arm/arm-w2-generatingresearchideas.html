ARM — Week 2: Generating Research Ideas

Focus

Generating a research idea is a gradual, iterative process, not a one-shot emotional pick. A good idea must survive a logical checklist across three categories — appropriateness (meeting requirements), capability (can you actually do it in 3 months), and personal interest — not "it sounds impressive". The deliverable this week is concrete: the Google Doc must contain an idea/problem, two research questions, aims, and a preliminary title by next class (~2026-05-27). The lecturer's blunt line: a good idea is almost the opposite of a complex-sounding one — if a normal person can't understand your title, that's a weakness, not a strength.

---

Key Concepts

  • Iterative idea generation — first idea ≠ final idea; if it doesn't change after reading papers, you either aren't learning or are doing it wrong
  • Three-category idea checklist — appropriateness, capability, personal interest (use a logical scoring table, not emotion)
  • Appropriateness / meeting requirements — assessment criteria, data rules, ethics, existing theory; violating these can zero a project regardless of quality
  • No Kaggle datasets — banned in DBS/GPS (some are manipulated/unverified); using one is an automatic bad idea
  • Existing theory required — if there's no prior literature/framework to build on, it fails requirements ("only 5 references" = bad idea)
  • Symmetry of potential outcomes — findings must be valuable regardless of result; guarantee usefulness before running anything
  • Fresh insights vs wholly new ideas — build on what exists; you're not expected to be Newton/Einstein
  • Capability — personal capability (know every algorithm you propose), resource availability, data access
  • Graded on a curve — distinctions are limited; play to documented strengths, not aspirational ones
  • Personal interest — genuine passion sustains 6 months and drives publication; but interest alone is not enough
  • Rational vs creative thinking — list grades/strengths, search literature, use staff research interests vs brainstorming, relevance tree, gap-spotting
  • Relevance tree — structured plan for the literature review, organised by research question (not random reading)
  • Gap-spotting — find a verified real gap in the literature, not a re-do of solved work
  • Research question vs aim vs objective — three distinct things (see below)
  • RQ count — 2 is the sweet spot (1 overarching + 1 focused); 3 is too many; 1 is borderline
  • Aims — 2–5 per RQ, each starts with an infinitive verb (to investigate / to assess / to measure)
  • Objectives — the actionable plan (no fixed limit); written after the literature review
  • Problem-first — define the problem before the title; "predict house prices" is not a problem
  • RQ/aim rubric — transparency, specificity, relevance, connectivity, answerability, measurability
  • Four levels of value (Myron Golden) — implementation < unification < communication < imagination
  • Don't use generative AI for research questions — ownership and understanding matter; they're 3 lines, it's lazy thinking to outsource them

Notes (live capture)

Idea generation is gradual and iterative:

  • You'll spend ~6 months on this — vet the idea, don't lock the first thought
  • After reading enough papers your idea should shift; supervision exists to clarify and reformulate
  • Done haphazardly = last-minute panic-pick; "if it's not taking time, you're doing it wrong"
  • Observation of the real world is a strong source (Newton's tree; a student's transit-card app from a real commuting pain) — when you've seen the problem, you don't have to argue it's real

Category 1 — Appropriateness / meeting requirements (most important, can zero you):

  • Actually read the CA brief and the marking criteria — know what an A requires before submitting
  • Data collection rules: no Kaggle datasets; company datasets allowed but must be discussed + guaranteed before a data-submission deadline
  • Surveys / human data → ethics approval required (lecturer → Andrew → ethics board) before collecting anything; can take 4–5 weeks; collecting without approval is serious (lawsuit-level risk)
  • Existing theory must exist around the idea; assessment-criteria weighting matters (e.g. if EDA is worth 50% and your data can't be explored, drop it)
  • Fresh insights, not wholly new ideas; ensure symmetry of outcomes — useful no matter the result

Category 2 — Capability (most ignored):

  • Students overestimate the long run and underestimate the short run
  • Personal capability: you must already know every algorithm in the proposal — expect to be quizzed live (PCA → eigenvectors/eigenvalues; random forest → hyperparameters, cross-validation). "I'll learn it when I need it" is the trap
  • Resource availability: no supercomputer, no project that needs one; if your machine can't handle the data, it's a great project for someone else
  • Data access: confirm company data early; surveys need early ethics lead time
  • Graded on a curve: someone who nails random forest can outscore your "better" LSTM idea — use your strengths

Category 3 — Personal interest:

  • Genuine interest sustains effort and shows in interviews ("you light up"); hated projects die by month 2
  • But interest alone ≠ good idea — it must also be appropriate and within capability
  • Sweet spot = where passion ∩ appropriateness ∩ ability meet; that's where you'd publish
  • Publication is a big CV signal: shows you take feedback, work in teams, write cleanly, and read what experts read

How to find ideas (rational + creative):

  • Rational: list your actual grades (numbers don't lie), read existing literature, mine staff research interests (pick a supervisor's field → they give insight from work they actually did)
  • Creative: read widely across fields, read last year's theses in the library, brainstorming (looks silly, but billion-dollar firms do it for hours), relevance tree, gap-spotting, LinkedIn/IEEE for current work
  • Talk to colleagues and tutors — a 5-minute conversation can save 3 weeks; saying the idea out loud improves it and exposes communication gaps early
  • Example failure a conversation would have caught: a regression project reported with accuracy/recall instead of RMSE — fixable in seconds if discussed early, fatal if discovered at the final presentation

Brainstorming exercise (done in class):

  • Write ~20 ideas, then rank them in a table on logical criteria (appropriateness, capability, interest) — the winner may not be the one you feel best about
  • Define the problem first, not the title: "predict stocks/house prices" is a task, not a problem. House-price prediction with one year of data is useless to a buyer who can just look it up; it only makes sense reframed (e.g. multi-year forecasting for investors/realtors)

Research question vs aim vs objective:

  • RQ — the question(s) you answer; ends in "?"; 2 is ideal (overarching + focused). Often phrased "to what extent…"
  • Aim — a concise "to …" statement of where you intend to land; 2–5 per RQ; starts with to investigate/assess/measure/examine/establish
  • Objective — the concrete plan ("get data, clean it, train, evaluate"); written after the lit review; will churn
  • Don't outsource RQs to ChatGPT — if the question isn't yours, you'll fall short on what it demands and can't defend it

RQ/aim grading rubric (what the lecturer literally marks on):

  • Transparency — self-explanatory to a non-specialist; avoid undefined acronyms; if they don't understand the aim, everything after is useless
  • Specificity — not vague (transparency and specificity pull against each other — balance them)
  • Relevance — the why / the problem must be visible, not assumed obvious
  • Connectivity — aims/RQs must connect into one coherent project (this is why 3+ RQs fragments the work)
  • Answerability — do you have the resources to answer it? ("Is there life on Mars?" is a fine question, unanswerable for you)
  • Measurability — is there evidence/metric showing the aim was achieved?

Four levels of value (Myron Golden) — why this module's "soft" skills pay:

  • Implementation (hands/doing the work) → lowest pay; unification (managing/leading) → higher; communication (moving an audience) → higher still; imagination (generating ideas/solving problems) → highest
  • The point: companies pay most for imagination + communication — coming up with a real idea, framing it, and communicating it (report, presentation, publication) beats "I can code faster than you"
  • The supervisor analogy: senior researchers' job is meetings, framing problems, and proposing solutions — not coding. Problem-solving = seeing past an obstacle others treat as fixed

Flashcards (SR)

  • Q: Why is idea generation described as iterative?

    • A: The first idea should not be the final one — reading papers should change it; if it doesn't, you're not learning or are picking haphazardly.

  • Q: What are the three categories a good idea must pass?

    • A: Appropriateness (meeting requirements), capability (can you actually do it), and personal interest — scored logically, not emotionally.

  • Q: Why are Kaggle datasets banned?

    • A: Some are manipulated/unverified; DBS/GPS disallows them, so using one is an automatic bad idea regardless of the work's quality.

  • Q: What is "symmetry of potential outcomes"?

    • A: The findings must be valuable regardless of the result — you guarantee usefulness before running the study, removing pressure to manipulate results.

  • Q: What does "capability" actually require of the student?

    • A: You must already understand every algorithm you propose (expect a live quiz), have the compute/resources, and have guaranteed data access.

  • Q: How many research questions, and in what structure?

    • A: Two — one overarching plus one focused. Three is too many (fragments the project); one is borderline.

  • Q: Distinguish research question, aim, and objective.

    • A: RQ = the question (ends in "?"); aim = a concise "to …" statement of intended achievement (2–5 per RQ); objective = the concrete actionable plan, written after the lit review.

  • Q: What six criteria does the lecturer mark research questions/aims on?

    • A: Transparency, specificity, relevance, connectivity, answerability, and measurability.

  • Q: Why define the problem before the title?

    • A: A title/task ("predict house prices") isn't a problem; without a real problem the prediction may be pointless (a buyer can just look the price up).

  • Q: Why not use generative AI to write research questions?

    • A: They're only ~3 lines and must be yours — outsourcing them means you can't fully understand or defend them, which is lazy thinking.

  • Q: What are the four levels of value, and which pay most?

    • A: Implementation < unification < communication < imagination; communication and imagination (idea generation/problem-solving) pay the most.
