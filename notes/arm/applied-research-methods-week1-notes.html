What The Module Is Really For

Applied Research Methods is preparation for the dissertation.

The lecturer's warning was blunt: students fail or struggle when they delay topic choice, pick techniques they do not understand, miss meetings, or treat the proposal as something to solve later. The goal is to leave this module with a proposal and working research file that make the dissertation executable.

The dissertation is not expected to invent a new algorithm. It is expected to apply known methods rigorously to a specific real-world problem where the value to stakeholders can be explained.

Basic vs Applied Research

Basic research:

  • Purpose: explore fundamental concepts, discover new phenomena, build or extend

    • theory, and create general knowledge.

  • Context: often academic, researcher-led, and more flexible in topic choice and

    • timeline.

  • Impact: usually starts in academia and may later influence industry, society,

    • or policy.

Applied research:

  • Purpose: solve a specific practical problem.
  • Context: often industry-facing, organization-facing, time-bound, and tied to

    • stakeholders.

  • Impact: should be useful to practitioners, managers, organizations, users, or

    • a defined community now, not only in a speculative future.

For Nathan's dissertation:

  • The work must be framed as applied research.
  • The practical problem is evaluator reliability for Portuguese academic RAG.
  • Stakeholders could include researchers, MSc students, academic-writing

    • support teams, Portuguese NLP practitioners, RAG system builders, and anyone evaluating grounded generated answers in Portuguese academic settings.

  • The value is not a new RAG architecture; the value is a defensible method for

    • comparing automatic grounding evaluators against human sentence-level labels.

Research Is Iterative

The slide process includes topic formulation, literature review, research design, data collection, analysis, and writing up. The lecturer emphasized that this is not linear in practice.

Expected iteration:

  • A topic idea may change after the literature review.
  • Research questions may change after checking feasibility.
  • Methodology may change after discovering data or annotation constraints.
  • A good student catches these problems early while there is still time.

Bad pattern:

  • Spend weeks thinking without committing.
  • Pick a topic because it sounds impressive.
  • Choose algorithms not understood.
  • Discover too late that the method or dataset is not viable.

Good pattern:

  • Pick a defensible idea early.
  • Read papers immediately.
  • Check stakeholder value.
  • Check method feasibility.
  • Revise early if needed.

Relevance Gap

The relevance gap is the separation between knowledge producers and the people who can use the knowledge.

In this module, the practical lesson is:

  • Research must be communicated clearly.
  • The user of the research must be identifiable.
  • The work should reduce a real gap between what is known in papers and what

    • practitioners can do.

  • A thesis that cannot answer "who would use this and why?" is weakly framed.

For Nathan's dissertation, the relevance-gap framing should be:

  • RAG evaluation methods exist, but their reliability for Portuguese academic

    • outputs is not straightforward for practitioners to trust.

  • The project reduces the gap by comparing automatic evaluator outputs with

    • human sentence-level judgments and documenting disagreement patterns.

Reflective Journal

The lecturer strongly recommended a reflective diary or research notebook.

Purpose:

  • Track weekly progress.
  • Record decisions, problems, and lessons.
  • Prepare for viva/defense questions.
  • Prevent self-deception about skipped weeks.
  • Build evidence of the research journey.

It can be informal. It can be short. The important thing is consistency.

Suggested weekly entry format:

Date:
What I worked on:
What went well:
Why it went well:
What went wrong or felt unclear:
Why it happened:
What this means for my research questions/method:
Decision or adjustment:
Next action:

Topic Selection Rules

Pick a topic early, ideally in the first 2-3 weeks.

A good applied research topic should:

  • Solve a specific practical problem.
  • Have identifiable stakeholders.
  • Have existing literature.
  • Use methods that can be learned and defended within the module timeline.
  • Be narrow enough for 6 months.
  • Avoid techniques chosen only because they sound impressive.

The lecturer's consultant analogy:

  • A consultant should know the tools, methods, timeline, risks, and expected

    • outputs before starting.

  • A student proposal should do the same.
  • Saying "I will figure out the algorithm next semester" is not a defensible

    • proposal stance.

For Nathan:

  • The topic is already stronger than a vague AI project because the title, RQs,

    • annotation budget, and evaluator set are locked.

  • The weak point to resolve next is corpus selection and proposal wording.
  • The method must be described as an applied evaluation study, not as a product

    • build or a broad RAG benchmark.

Tsui's Responsible Research Principles

The slides list seven responsible-research principles:

  1. Stakeholder involvement.
  2. Valuing plurality and collaboration.
  3. Valuing basic and applied contributions.
  4. Improving rigor and relevance.
  5. Impact on stakeholders.
  6. Broad dissemination.
  7. Sound methodology.

Use these as a proposal checklist:

  • Who benefits from the dissertation?
  • What practical problem does it address?
  • What existing theory and prior research does it build on?
  • Is the method rigorous enough to be credible?
  • Is the output useful outside the classroom?
  • How will results be communicated clearly?
  • Are the data, annotation, evaluation, and analysis methods defensible?
