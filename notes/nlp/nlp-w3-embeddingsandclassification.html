NLP — Week 3: Bag-of-Words → Embeddings, BERT, and Text Classification

Focus

This lecture moved from sparse bag-of-words to dense distributed embeddings (Word2Vec, GloVe, BERT) and then into the classification half of CA1. The throughline: BoW/TF-IDF is the cheap, interpretable benchmark, but it loses meaning ("bank" gets one vector regardless of context); embeddings put semantically similar words near each other in continuous space (king − man + woman ≈ queen), and contextual transformer embeddings (BERT) fix the polysemy problem by reading left and right. For CA1 Task 2 you benchmark against AI Studio's TF-IDF and then build a grid of ≥3 representations × ≥3 algorithms (≥9 models) under CRISP-DM, always reporting per-class metrics. The lecturer also laid out the rest of the module — topic modelling, co-occurrence graphs + Neo4j knowledge graphs, RAG vs GraphRAG, and building an LLM from scratch — much of which is directly dissertation-relevant.

Key Concepts

  • Distributed / dense embedding — a learned low-dimensional vector per token where similar meanings cluster in space; you choose the dimensionality, so vectors are dense (not sparse like BoW).
  • Word2Vec — neural-network embedding (Mikolov et al., 2013, Google). Two variants: CBOW (predict the target word from surrounding context) and skip-gram (predict surrounding context from the target word). Local context only (~5-word window).
  • GloVe — Global Vectors (Pennington et al., 2014, Stanford). Count/co-occurrence based, not predictive: factorises a global word–co-occurrence matrix; relates the dot product of two word vectors to the log of their co-occurrence probability. Captures global + local statistics; slower but often more accurate than Word2Vec.
  • Static vs contextual embeddings — Word2Vec/GloVe are static (one fixed vector per word, ignores sense); BERT is contextual (the vector for "bank" depends on the sentence).
  • BERT — Bidirectional Encoder Representations from Transformers (Devlin et al., 2018, Google). Uses the encoder half of the transformer; self-attention reads context in both directions. Trained with MLM (masked language modelling) + NSP (next-sentence prediction). Used for embeddings and classification.
  • DistilBERT — smaller/faster distilled BERT; the practical choice when full BERT is too heavy for experiments.
  • Self-attention — weighs the importance of every other token when forming a token's representation ("attention is all you need"). Wide context vs Word2Vec/GloVe's local window.
  • Transfer learning (for text) — pre-train on a huge corpus, then fine-tune on a small labelled set; brings the image-transfer-learning idea into NLP. Fewer labelled examples needed.
  • Out-of-vocabulary (OOV) problem — static embeddings have no vector for words unseen in training.
  • Co-occurrence network/matrix — two word-nodes linked by how often they co-occur within a window; underlies GloVe, t-SNE/UMAP visualisation, and Louvain-based topic modelling.
  • VADER — rule-based sentiment scorer; can auto-label unlabelled text with a sentiment score you then threshold into classes.
  • Naive Bayes — classic, strong baseline for spam (the original spam filters were Bayesian).
  • CRISP-DM — the 6-stage report scaffold for CA1 (and the client research project): Business Understanding → Data Understanding → Data Preparation → Modelling → Evaluation → Deployment.

Notes (live capture)

BoW recap & why we move on:

  • TF-IDF (TfidfVectorizer) is the recommended benchmark and what AI Studio runs (top 1000 dimensions). CountVectorizer is the simpler frequency baseline — sometimes fine when class vocabularies are obviously distinct (e.g. medicines vs buildings).
  • Hyperparameters live in the vectoriser brackets: max_features (dimensions), ngram_range, stop_words, lowercasing; plus stemming/lemmatisation upstream.
  • Stemming vs lemmatisation: stemming crudely chops suffixes ("caring"→"car"); lemmatisation returns a dictionary root ("caring"→"care"). Lemmatisation is slower but more interpretable.
  • BoW limits: no word order ("dog bites man" = "man bites dog"), loses context/nuance, high-dimensional sparse vectors → curse of dimensionality.
  • Leakage discipline: fit the vectoriser on training data only; transform test data using the training vocabulary.

Embeddings intuition:

  • Map words into a continuous vector space where similar meanings congregate; you can do arithmetic on the vectors (king − man + woman ≈ queen — from the Word2Vec paper).
  • Project high-dim embeddings to 2D for visualisation with t-SNE (or PCA when the first 2 components capture >~95% variance). Capitals cluster near their countries; tenses/grammatical variants cluster.
  • Benefits: dimensionality reduction (you pick the size), semantic information retained, transfer learning across tasks.

Word2Vec vs GloVe:

  • Word2Vec — neural, local context window (~5 words). CBOW = context→target; skip-gram = target→context (good for rare words).
  • GloVe — global co-occurrence matrix factorisation; takes longer to train but folds in corpus-wide statistics. (Lecturer said GloVe "predicts context words" — it's actually count/co-occurrence based, not predictive; see flag.)
  • Both are static: the word "bank" (river vs financial) gets the same vector → motivation for BERT.

BERT:

  • Encoder side of the transformer; bidirectional self-attention → context-aware embeddings, so "bank" differs by sentence.
  • Pre-trained on Wikipedia + BookCorpus via MLM (mask a token, predict it from both sides — e.g. mask "fox" in "the quick brown ___ jumps…") and NSP (does sentence B follow sentence A?).
  • Fine-tune by adding a task-specific layer (e.g. a classification head) on top and training on a small labelled set.
  • BERT (or DistilBERT) often beats traditional methods at classification, especially meaning-heavy tasks (sentiment, emotion, fake news, bias). Anecdote: a student did emotion detection with DistilBERT after traditional algorithms underperformed.

Classification (CA1 Task 2):

  • Once vectorised, it's standard ML: split → balance training data (lecturer prefers undersampling when you have enough data; class weighting and SMOTE often disappoint on BoW) → cross-validate + hyperparameter grid search.
  • 12-fold CV question: which of the 12 models is returned? A 13th, trained on all the data — the folds exist to estimate the confidence interval (mean ± std). AI Studio uses 3-fold.
  • Dimensionality control: cap max_features or run TF-IDF → PCA/SVD (PCA is a special case of SVD). Anecdote: TF-IDF → PCA → Random Forest won a fake-news task.
  • Sentiment is hard (sarcasm/context). VADER can auto-label unlabelled text with a sentiment score you then threshold.
  • Naive Bayes = useful spam baseline. SVM and deep learning often top the table; deep learning tends to win meaning-heavy problems.
  • Real systems often stack multiple classifiers (age, gender, topic…) feeding a final decision.

Evaluation discipline (repeated, exam-worthy):

  • Always report per-class precision/recall/F1 (classification_report) — never a bare accuracy or only the weighted average; they hide poor minority-class performance.
  • AUC ≥ 0.8 ≈ a good model. Also report time-to-train / time-to-test — a real deployment cost (a heavy transformer may not be worth it over a count vectoriser on an easy problem).
  • "No free lunch": no representation/algorithm wins universally — experiment. The whole crux of CA1 is which representation best captures meaning for this data.

Dataset constraint (do not ignore):

  • CA1 Task 2 dataset must be BLOG GENDER or the Clinician/MIMIC ICD disease-code discharge notes — not an arbitrary dataset. A prior student lost marks for choosing arbitrarily. MIMIC needs access approval + download (you must do it yourself; takes a while).
  • Baleen corpus is a nice hand-labelled topic dataset (cinema, cooking, DIY, gaming, sports, tech, book) — useful for topic classification and as a truth benchmark for topic-modelling comparisons.

Library cheat-sheet:

  • scikit-learn → CountVectorizer, TfidfVectorizer (BoW); lecturer's preferred, cleanest API.
  • gensim → Word2Vec (pip install gensim; not pre-installed in Colab); tokenise with NLTK first.
  • transformers → BertTokenizer + BertModel: tokenise, then pull the embedding array (array-of-arrays, one vector per token) — the same two-step shape GPT will use.

# --- BERT embeddings (Transformers) ---
from transformers import BertTokenizer, BertModel
tok = BertTokenizer.from_pretrained("bert-base-uncased")
bert = BertModel.from_pretrained("bert-base-uncased")

Flashcards (SR)

  • Q: CBOW vs skip-gram?

    • A: CBOW predicts the target word from its context; skip-gram predicts the context from the target word (better for rare words). Both are Word2Vec, neural, local-window.

  • Q: Word2Vec vs GloVe in one line?

    • A: Word2Vec = neural, local context; GloVe = global co-occurrence-matrix factorisation (count-based). GloVe slower, often more accurate.

  • Q: Why does BERT solve the "bank" problem when Word2Vec/GloVe don't?

    • A: BERT is contextual + bidirectional (self-attention reads both sides) → different vectors per sense; Word2Vec/GloVe are static (one vector per word).

  • Q: BERT's two pre-training objectives?

    • A: MLM (mask a token, predict it from both directions) and NSP (does sentence B follow A?).

  • Q: 12-fold CV — which model is returned?

    • A: A 13th, trained on all the data; the 12 folds give the confidence interval (mean ± std).

  • Q: How do you balance and validate for CA1 Task 2?

    • A: Undersample the majority class (preferred when data is sufficient); StratifiedKFold CV + GridSearchCV; fit vectoriser on train only.

  • Q: What metric must you always report — and what must you not rely on?

    • A: Report per-class precision/recall/F1 (+ AUC, train/test time). Don't rely on bare/averaged accuracy.

  • Q: Allowed CA1 Task 2 datasets?

    • A: BLOG GENDER or Clinician/MIMIC ICD disease-code discharge notes — not arbitrary.

Flags / Corrections

  • Attribution muddle: BERT ≠ "Attention Is All You Need." The lecturer attributed BERT to "Baswani/Swani et al. 2017." Correct: the transformer architecture (encoder+decoder) is Vaswani et al., 2017 ("Attention Is All You Need"); BERT is Devlin et al., 2018 (Google), using only the encoder. GPT uses the decoder.
