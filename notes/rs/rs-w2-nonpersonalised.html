RS — Week 2: Non-Personalised Recommenders

Focus

Non-personalised recommenders use collective signals — aggregate opinion, product association, and item content — instead of individual user history. They are the primary tool for cold start and the foundation for understanding ranking trade-offs (popularity, freshness, controversy).

Key Concepts

  • Non-personalised recommenders — same recommendations for all users, based on group signals
  • Aggregate opinion recommenders — based on preferences from all users
  • Product association recommenders — "people who bought X also bought Y"
  • Popularity models — wisdom of the crowd; useful for cold start
  • Matthew effect — popular items accumulate more recommendations, compounding popularity
  • Wilson score lower bound — balances positive proportion with sample-size uncertainty
  • Ranking trade-offs — relevance, freshness, controversy, business objectives
  • HackerNews / Reddit / IMDb / TripAdvisor ranking formulas — real-world popularity ranking

Notes (live capture)

Personalised vs non-personalised:

  • Personalised — different recommendations per user, based on individual history/behaviour
  • Non-personalised — same output for all users; uses general item, group, or popularity signals

Three non-personalised types:

  • Aggregate opinion — based on ratings/preferences from the full user base
  • Product association — item-to-item co-occurrence ("frequently bought together")
  • Content-based (non-personalised variant) — based on item attributes, no user model needed

Why popularity models work:

  • Wisdom-of-the-crowd effect
  • People often follow group trends (social, regional, platform-level)
  • Useful for cold start because no individual history needed

Popularity model drawbacks:

  • Matthew effect: popular → more popular, new/niche items cannot surface
  • Region/language mismatch for new users or travellers
  • Can be gamed or manipulated
  • Political/ideological/controversial content can exploit engagement signals
  • Systems must be designed with abuse and user-harm risks in mind

Why single metrics fail:

  • Mean rating hides polarised distributions
  • Positive percentage over-credits small sample sizes (5 ratings at 100% beats 5,000 at 99%?)
  • Raw total score over-credits high-volume but mediocre items
  • Number of views/likes hides dislikes

Ranking considerations:

  • Relevance — is it close to what the user/group expects?
  • Conservative vs risky recommendations — safe personalisation vs novel/sponsored/business-driven
  • Freshness — critical in news; stale content should decay
  • Controversy — drives engagement but can mislead ranking signal

Wilson score:

  • Asks: given the observed ratings, what lower-bound positive proportion can we be confident about?
  • Balances proportion with uncertainty from limited sample size
  • Example: Item A 600 pos / 400 neg (60%); Item B 5,500 pos / 4,500 neg (55%)
  • Raw score → Item B wins; Wilson lower bound → Item A wins (more confidence per observation)

Other ranking formulas (conceptual):

  • HackerNews — upvotes + downvotes + age + gravity/decay factor
  • Reddit — upvotes, downvotes, log scaling, time; high upvotes + high downvotes = controversial
  • IMDb Top 250 — weighted rating: average rating, vote count, minimum-vote threshold, global mean
  • TripAdvisor — review quality, quantity, and recency
  • Google PageRank — mentioned as a later algorithmic reference

Math / Code

import math

def wilson_lower_bound(pos: int, total: int, confidence: float = 0.95) -> float:
    """
    Wilson score lower bound for a Bernoulli parameter.
    pos   = number of positive ratings
    total = total number of ratings
    Returns the lower bound of the confidence interval.
    """
    if total == 0:
        return 0.0
    z = 1.96  # 95% confidence; use 1.645 for 90%
    p_hat = pos / total
    numerator = p_hat + z**2 / (2 * total) - z * math.sqrt(
        (p_hat * (1 - p_hat) + z**2 / (4 * total)) / total
    )
    denominator = 1 + z**2 / total
    return numerator / denominator

# Example from lecture
item_a = wilson_lower_bound(600, 1000)   # 60% positive, n=1000
item_b = wilson_lower_bound(5500, 10000) # 55% positive, n=10000
print(f"Item A: {item_a:.4f}, Item B: {item_b:.4f}")
# Item A wins on Wilson lower bound despite lower raw proportion

Flashcards (SR)

  • Q: What are the three non-personalised recommender types?

    • A: Aggregate opinion (based on all users' preferences), product association (item co-occurrence), and content-based (based on item attributes with no user model).

  • Q: Why can a 100% positive rating be less trustworthy than a 99% positive rating?

    • A: Sample size matters — 100% from 3 ratings is statistically weak; 99% from 10,000 ratings is far more reliable. Wilson score lower bound accounts for this.

  • Q: What does the Wilson score lower bound fix compared to raw positive percentage?

    • A: It balances the observed positive proportion with uncertainty from limited sample size — asking what positive proportion we can be confidently sure of, not just what we observed.

  • Q: Why does cold start make popularity models useful?

    • A: Cold start means no individual history exists; popularity models use group/collective signals that do not require individual data.

  • Q: What is the Matthew effect in recommender systems?

    • A: Popular items get recommended more → more interactions → more popular. New or niche items cannot compete, reinforcing existing popularity.

  • Q: Why might a business intentionally include risky or sponsored recommendations?

    • A: Revenue objectives, partnerships, or promotional contracts may override pure user-preference optimisation — business context shapes ranking decisions.

  • Q: What is the difference between a conservative and a risky recommendation?

    • A: Conservative = close to what the user or similar group is expected to like. Risky = diverse, novel, controversial, or business-driven, with higher uncertainty about reception.

  • Q: Name three real-world ranking systems and the key signal each uses.

    • A: IMDb — weighted rating with minimum vote threshold; HackerNews — upvotes + time decay; TripAdvisor — review quality, quantity, and recency.
