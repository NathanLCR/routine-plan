Week 1: Introduction

Definition:

  • Recommender systems are software tools and techniques that suggest items best

    • suited to a user's interests.

  • The system is normally designed around a specific item type: books, movies,

    • restaurants, products, songs, videos, jobs, courses, or similar.

Purpose:

  • Reduce information overload.
  • Reduce decision fatigue.
  • Help users evaluate alternatives when they lack enough knowledge or time.

Business benefits:

  • Customer loyalty.
  • Customer trust.
  • Cross-selling.
  • Increased sales.
  • Better customer understanding.

Core data types:

  • Item data: cost, features, genre, length, setting, benefit, text, tags, or

    • other descriptors.

  • User profile data: age, sex, location, profession, preferences, ratings, and

    • inferred user model.

  • Explicit preferences: ratings, stars, likes, survey answers, votes, reviews.
  • Implicit preferences: browsing patterns, clicked links, watched videos,

    • listened songs, time spent, repeat visits, purchases.

Core recommender families introduced:

  • Collaborative filtering: recommend based on similar users or similar user-item

    • behavior.

  • Content-based recommendations: recommend items similar to item features or

    • descriptions.

  • Socio-demographic recommendations: recommend using demographic attributes such

    • as age, location, profession, income, or gender.

  • Knowledge-based recommendations: recommend using explicit domain constraints

    • and user requirements.

  • Conversational recommendations: use dialogue to elicit preferences.

Collaborative filtering flow:

  1. Calculate similarity between a target user and other users.
  2. Form a neighborhood of similar users.
  3. Predict the target user's rating for an item by aggregating neighbor ratings.

Cold start:

  • Cold start occurs when there is not enough user, item, rating, or interaction

    • history to make informed personalized recommendations.

  • Example: a new Netflix user has no watch history, so the system may begin with

    • popular or regional trends.

Evaluation:

  • Offline evaluation uses train/test splits and prediction/recommendation

    • metrics before deployment.

  • Online evaluation or user studies measure satisfaction, interaction, adoption,

    • and real-world impact.

Ethical risks:

  • Binge-watching.
  • Overconsumption.
  • Overspending.
  • Mindless scrolling.
  • Idea segregation.
  • Privacy concerns.

Week 1 ML Refresher

The lecturer emphasized that recommender work still depends on basic ML judgment:

  • Choose supervised learning when a target variable exists.
  • Use regression when the outcome is numerical.
  • Use classification when the outcome is categorical.
  • Use unsupervised learning when labels are absent or when discovering hidden

    • clusters such as customer segments.

  • Deep learning is a different learning architecture with input, hidden, and

    • output layers; it can still be used for regression, classification, or unsupervised tasks.

Data/model checks:

  • Use EDA before modeling.
  • Check missing values, duplicates, distributions, and variable relationships.
  • Use correlation heatmaps to inspect whether numeric features relate linearly

    • to the target and whether features are redundant with each other.

  • Avoid collinearity, such as using height, weight, and BMI together without

    • justification.

  • If the target is numeric but relationships are not linear, consider

    • non-linear regressors such as decision-tree regressors or SVM regressors.

Regression evaluation:

  • Error metrics: MAE, MSE, RMSE.
  • Variance explained: R-squared.
  • Interpret error in business/domain context, not personal intuition.

Classification evaluation:

  • Confusion matrix: true positives, true negatives, false positives, false

    • negatives.

  • Metrics: accuracy, precision, recall, F1, AUC-ROC.
  • Which error matters depends on context.
  • Healthcare example: false negatives can be more critical when a real disease

    • is missed.

  • Fraud example: recall is often more important than raw accuracy.

Other practical ML issues:

  • Overfitting: model learns training data too closely.
  • Underfitting: model learns too little.
  • Mitigations include cross-validation, hyperparameter tuning, grid search, and

    • random search.

  • Standardization/normalization matter for distance-based methods such as

    • clustering and KNN.

  • Fit scalers on training data only to avoid test-data leakage.
  • For classification, check class imbalance and consider oversampling,

    • undersampling, or SMOTE-like methods where suitable.

Week 2: Non-Personalized Recommenders

Personalized vs non-personalized:

  • Personalized recommenders make different recommendations for each user based

    • on individual characteristics, preferences, and behavior.

  • Non-personalized recommenders do not rely on individual user history; they use

    • general item, group, popularity, or aggregate behavior signals.

Three non-personalized types:

  • Aggregate opinion recommenders: based on preferences from all users.
  • Product association recommenders: based on relations between items, such as

    • "people who bought a laptop also bought a laptop bag."

  • Content-based recommenders: based on descriptions or attributes of items.

Popularity models:

  • Useful for cold start because they do not require individual user history.
  • Work partly because of the wisdom-of-the-crowd effect.
  • People often follow group trends across social, work, class, regional, or

    • platform communities.

Popularity-model drawbacks:

  • Matthew effect: popular items become more popular.
  • New or niche items struggle to surface.
  • Popular content may not be relevant to everyone.
  • Region and language mismatch can occur for new users or travelers.
  • Trend behavior can be manipulated.
  • Political, ideological, or controversial content can exploit engagement

    • ranking.

  • Systems should be designed with abuse and user-harm risks in mind.

Week 2 Ranking Ideas

Single metrics are often insufficient:

  • Mean rating can hide polarized distributions.
  • Percentage of positive ratings can over-credit tiny sample sizes.
  • Raw total score can over-credit high-volume but mediocre items.
  • Number of likes/views/purchases shows popularity but hides dislikes.
  • Full rating distributions are richer but harder to compare.

Ranking considerations:

  • Relevance to users.
  • Conservative recommendations: close to what the user or similar group is

    • expected to like.

  • Risky recommendations: more diverse, novel, controversial, sponsored, or

    • business-driven recommendations.

  • Business objectives may promote certain items even if they are not the safest

    • user-personalized recommendation.

  • Freshness matters in domains such as news.
  • Controversy can drive engagement and influence ranking.

Wilson score:

  • Wilson score lower bound balances positive-rating proportion with uncertainty

    • from limited sample size.

  • It asks: given the ratings observed, what lower-bound positive proportion can

    • we be reasonably confident about?

  • Week 2 example:
  • Item 1: 600 positive, 400 negative, 60% positive.
  • Item 2: 5,500 positive, 4,500 negative, 55% positive.
  • Raw score favors Item 2, but Wilson lower bound favors Item 1.

Other ranking examples:

  • HackerNews-style ranking combines upvotes, downvotes, submission age, and a

    • gravity/decay factor.

  • Reddit-style ranking uses upvotes, downvotes, time, and logarithmic scaling.
  • Downvotes complicate controversial stories because high upvotes and high

    • downvotes may signal attention rather than low relevance.

  • IMDb Top 250 uses a weighted-rating idea with average rating, vote count,

    • minimum-vote threshold, and global mean rating.

  • TripAdvisor rankings consider review quality, quantity, and recency.
  • Google PageRank was mentioned as a later algorithmic reference.
